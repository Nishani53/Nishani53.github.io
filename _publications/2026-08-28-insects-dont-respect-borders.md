This opinion article examines the rising threat of spruce budworm populations in Maine and Atlantic Canada, arguing that effective pest mitigation requires treating the Acadian forest as a unified ecosystem across international boundaries.

## Summary

The piece addresses how political borders fail to stop moth dispersal across border corridors and calls for unified monitoring, compatible treatment thresholds, and streamlined regional emergency response protocols.

### Key Discussion Points:
* **Biological Reality vs. Administrative Lines:** Spruce budworm moths disperse long distances via atmospheric wind currents, rendering isolated state or provincial treatment strategies ineffective against regional outbreaks.
* **Lessons from Historical Outbreaks:** Highlights the severe damage from the 1967–1993 outbreak, which impacted 2.8 million hectares in Maine and peak footprints of 55 million hectares across eastern Canada.
* **Proven Containment Strategies:** Cites the success of New Brunswick's Early Intervention Strategy (EIS) and recent 2025 Maine treatment results that reduced target populations by roughly 95% using targeted biological insecticides.
* **Economic Stakes:** Connects forest health directly to regional economies—protecting Maine's $11B+ forest sector and over $2B in economic activity supporting 16,600 jobs across Atlantic Canada.
* **Framework for Cross-Border Action:** Proposes four synchronized measures: shared pheromone trapping data, unified satellite mapping, compatible intervention thresholds, and standing emergency resource-sharing agreements.

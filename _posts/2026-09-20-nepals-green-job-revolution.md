Nepal’s recent political shift reflects a deeper frustration among young voters with the country’s old development model. Across the country, a new generation is demanding more than slogans, patronage politics, and short-term infrastructure projects. They want decent jobs, functioning institutions, and a reason to stay in Nepal instead of searching for opportunities abroad. But if Nepal’s next phase of development ignores climate resilience and the economic potential of its forests, the promise of “New Nepal” risks becoming another unfinished political slogan.

Nepal’s forests are not merely environmental assets; they are economic infrastructure. At a time when climate risks are growing and thousands of young Nepalis still leave the country for work each year, forests could support green industries, rural livelihoods, carbon finance, sustainable timber production, and even technology-driven jobs. Yet Nepal still treats forestry as a peripheral sector rather than a foundation for long-term economic resilience.

## The Peril of "Gray" Infrastructure in a Warming World

Road networks and hydropower are essential, but climate change makes these massive investments risky. In the Everest region alone, Glacial Lake Outburst Floods (GLOFs) pose a threat to thousands of buildings, dozens to hundreds of kilometers of roads and trails, and cascading disasters. Degraded watersheds exacerbate the harm: landslides destroy bridges, silt up hydro-tunnels, and cause billions of dollars' worth of losses. Climate resilience is a crucial component of economic risk management, and Nepal can no longer afford to treat it as an option given the decline in dependable foreign aid for recovery due to shifting global politics.

## Ending the Absurdity of Timber Imports Amid Untapped Wealth

Nepal has forest cover of almost 45%, but we do not use our forests at all; we use a "look but do not touch" approach. This type of thinking has caused forest-dwelling communities to stay impoverished, while the forests are filled with usable timber. 

The current system is an economic paradox. According to the Department of Customs, Nepal spent a staggering Rs 7.4 billion in FY 2021/22 to import wood and wood products, with over a billion rupees going to Indonesia alone. Meanwhile, tens of thousands of cubic meters of timber are harvested and unsold in the Lumbini province mainly due to low demand, high taxes, and other bureaucratic bottlenecks. Forestry experts estimate that improved timber management could generate an additional 30 billion rupees in revenue from 120 million cubic feet of timber while meeting domestic demand.

## Forests as Economic Assets in the Carbon Era

Today, forests are no longer just a source of wood, they are vital global resources. On January 23, 2026, Nepal made history by becoming the first country in Asia to sign a groundbreaking agreement with the LEAF Coalition. This agreement brings up to US$55 million in climate finance to help reduce emissions in the provinces of Gandaki, Bagmati, and Lumbini. Crucially, the agreement mandates that 80% of these funds directly benefit local communities, proving that conservation can generate real, decentralized wealth.

For a tech-savvy Gen Z, the forest sector offers opportunities far beyond traditional manual labor. The future lies in "agropreneurs" scaling high-value Non-Timber Forest Products (NTFPs), AI-powered digital monitoring systems for forest fire detection, and carbon market specialists managing global portfolios.

## De-Bureaucratizing the Green Revolution

The largest obstacle, however, remains bureaucracy. Reducing bureaucratic inefficiency was central to Balen’s political message. The community forestry program of Nepal has fallen prey to red tape, rent seeking, and antiquated processes. The new government must take this opportunity to implement a technology driven overhaul; automating inventory, approving operations and transferring revenues will remove gatekeeping and create a clear system of transparency. 

By establishing a digital pipeline for climate finance, we can prevent the 30% administrative overhead leakage that often plagues these funds, ensuring a clear chain of custody. In addition, the youth should have a greater role in Community Forest User Groups (CFUG) by removing barriers that restrict them from assuming leadership positions and end the practice of appointing political activists to these roles. The future of our forests rests with the generations who will inherit them.

## A Mandate for True Transformation

The 2025–2026 Gen Z wave demanded a rethinking of Nepal's future in addition to new leaders. I implore the Balen administration to fill the Ministry of Forests and Environment with progressive, science-driven leaders. Forests should no longer be treated as remnants of the past. Rather, acknowledge them as the foundation of the country's prosperity: generating green jobs, strengthening climate resilience, and converting natural resources into opportunities for all.

Nepal is at a critical juncture. Let's build the "New Nepal" on the living, breathing foundation of our forests rather than just on concrete and laws. Nepal’s young people deserve a development model built for the future.

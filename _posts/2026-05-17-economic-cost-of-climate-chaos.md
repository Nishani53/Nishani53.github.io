The alerts issued by the U.S. National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) in mid-May 2026 are no mere environmental alerts. Rather, they strike a blow to Nepal’s financial base. According to the NOAA predictions, there is an 82% likelihood of the development of a powerful El Niño by July. The United Nations Economic and Social Commission for Asia and the Pacific (ESCAP) has made a different alert about the instability of the monsoon season in South Asia. Together, these signals spell trouble that goes well beyond flooded fields and displaced families. They threaten the country's balance sheet.

For years, Singha Durbar has been managing climate change as an environmental portfolio; a few rescue helicopters here, some relief funds there. That position is now economically unsustainable. Violent weather doesn’t just destroy communities. It blows holes in the national accounts, widens the fiscal deficit and destabilizes the financial system. “Disasters are unpredictable acts of God” is a convenient lie. Natural resource economics is clear: if you ignore the erosion of natural capital, you are building vulnerability directly into your growth model. That is what Nepal has been doing for decades.

## Agriculture Choked; Forex Reserves Bled

Let’s begin with the numbers. Agriculture is estimated to be around 24 percent of the country's GDP, and yet, the National Statistics Office reports that agricultural growth was stuck at 1.58 percent. The main reason for this is a 4.16 percent decline in paddy production, a consequence of increasingly volatile climate.

The subsequent arithmetic is gruesome. When rain fails or appears as devastating unseasonal floods, the country heads to import markets for the food deficit. Import expenditure increases and the country’s foreign exchange reserves decline. Thus, the monsoon failure is not an environmental phenomenon but one that directly reduces dollar reserves and reduces room to maneuver for the central bank.

## The Hydropower Paradox Becomes a Banking Problem

The problem does not remain confined to farms but spreads very quickly to finance, becoming systemic. The hydropower industry in Nepal has been doing exceptionally well for the year 2025/26, with close to 385 MW added to the country’s energy grid in its initial months. However, what follows is something else, a structural paradox. The great majority of these projects are run-of-the-river projects whose energy production is a function of steady streamflow. In case of low river discharge due to El Niño effect, there would be a sudden drop in energy production.

It is no longer only an energy security issue. Nepal’s commercial banks are exposed to hydropower investments worth billions of rupees. In case of any weather phenomenon leading to lower river discharge, power plants will be unable to meet their financial obligations and thus, convert performing loans into non-performing assets. Bank liquidity will be affected, and credit provision to other sectors of the economy will be slowed. The transmission of weather events into banking problems is not hypothetical. It is the architecture Nepal has built.

## Eleven Billion Rupees Washed Away

There is a record of the physical destruction; extremely bad weather events, especially in October 2025, caused over Rs 11.81 billion worth of immediate damage in total to the eastern districts of Nepal; crops were wiped out and municipal infrastructures were decimated. In the same year, the Melamchi Drinking Water Project was also damaged, and it has been repaired and rebuilt so many times it has become an embarrassment for policymakers. This represents a highly visible example of a negative externality. The costs of global emissions are being offloaded onto Nepal’s domestic budget without compensation; Kathmandu did not create this disaster and yet, is responsible for the cost.

Without a complete change of direction, the maths of the State’s finances leads only to one place, an endless cycle of debt-financed reconstruction. The State will borrow money to rebuild, and then another major disaster will occur, and the State will need to borrow money again, and capital that ought to have been used for human development will just be recycled endlessly simply to fix the damage done.

## Three Concrete Breaks from the Cycle

The Ministry of Finance and the National Planning Commission can no longer act as if this is somebody else’s problem. Three instruments are essential:

1. **Sovereign Disaster-Risk Insurance:** Nepal needs to tap international reinsurance markets and get rid of catastrophic risks from the national balance sheet. If the next shock occurs, funds for reconstruction must come in through insurance claims, not through ad hoc debt finance or sudden revisions to the budget.
2. **Municipal Green Bonds:** Nepal needs a financing tool that will be associated with climate-resistant infrastructure including hardened water supply systems, climate-smart irrigation schemes, and flood-resistant roads. This eliminates reliance on risky flows of cash from the national treasury.
3. **Environmental Asset Accounting:** Nepal cannot go on considering its forests, watersheds, and soil health as externalities. The National Planning Commission needs to develop economic valuation of its natural assets to price in climate risk during infrastructure planning rather than after flooding occurs.

## Conclusion

El Niño is a deadline, not a warning. Climate policy is macroeconomic policy, and Nepal cannot balance its books while pretending the sky is irrelevant. The Rs 6.6 trillion economy cannot absorb another cycle of reactive governance. The helicopter rescues, the relief tents, and the emergency borrowing—none of it is a strategy. It is a habit. And it is one Nepal can no longer afford.

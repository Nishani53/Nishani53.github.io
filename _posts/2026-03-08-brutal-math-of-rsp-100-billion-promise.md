The dust has settled, and the results of the general elections held on the 5th of March are out. And the message from the Nepali voter is clear: the era of the old guard is over. Not only has the Rastriya Swatantra Party (RSP) won, but it has also been given a mandate for a revolutionary shift in the Nepali economy. Balen Shah has defeated the old guard by a record margin, and the "Gen Z uprising" that started on the streets has finally reached the corridors of Singha Durbar.

But the new leadership must deal with a sobering reality as the victory rallies wane. The "Citizen Contract" manifesto of the RSP is arguably the most ambitious economic plan in Nepal's recent history. Within five years, it promises to nearly double per capita income to $3,000, double the country's GDP to $100 billion, and generate 1.2 million formal jobs. Even though these numbers have inspired a disillusioned youth, political wills alone won't be enough to turn a $45 billion economy into a $100 billion powerhouse. The extractive structures that have historically impeded our progress must be surgically dismantled.

## The Math of the Mandate

This is the hard math that the new government must deal with: Nepal needs a startling Compound Annual Growth Rate (CAGR) of more than 17.3% to reach a $100 billion GDP in five years from the current $45 billion baseline. To put this in perspective, we have only averaged 4.2% growth over the past ten years. In our environment of persistent project delays, where the government frequently finds it difficult to spend even 50% of its allotted capital expenditure by the end of the fiscal year, achieving 17% growth is an unprecedented achievement.

In its "100 Pillars of Policy Departure," the RSP identifies information technology (IT), clean energy, and domestic production as the key growth drivers of the new economy. While the goal of exporting $30 billion worth of IT services within ten years is ambitious, it can only be achieved through the successful implementation of the "Digital Nepal" initiative that goes beyond mere slogans. To accomplish this, Nepal needs to immediately remove all barriers to overseas foreign direct investment (FDI) through repealing restrictive laws and removing bureaucratic obstacles that create a prohibitively high "cost to do business."

## Beyond the Remittance Trap

Perhaps the most important measure of the success or failure of the RSP will be whether it can halt the demographic hemorrhage afflicting our nation. Today, remittances contribute 25% to our GDP, essentially an economic lifeline but also "lazy capital" that goes into imports and consumption rather than domestic investment.

The RSP’s promise of 1.2 million jobs is the only way to halt the daily loss of 3,000 youths leaving the country, a staggering figure comprising about 2,000 daily labor permits, alongside a massive exodus of students and informal cross border migrants. To achieve this, the government must look beyond the elation of winning an election.

The government should take an aggressive approach to eliminate predatory lending networks (*meter byaj*) and reforming shadow banking in cooperative sector under a strict regulatory framework similar to the central bank's. By releasing frozen capital in these sectors for sustainable energy projects and value-added agricultural products, reaching the goal of $100 billion will change from being just words on a piece of paper to being mathematically possible.

## The Challenge of Syndication

In the end, the entrenched bureaucratic syndicates will be the RSP's biggest adversary rather than the vanquished political ones. Everything is controlled by protectionist cartels, including public procurement and transportation logistics. The party's plan to appoint subject-matter experts and restrict the number of federal ministries to eighteen is a good place to start. However, as any institutional economist knows, the game's rules are far more important than the resources at hand.

Nepal's difficulty in attracting foreign investment and trade is primarily due to a history of chronic instability in the policies that regulate these activities. Tax rates and other regulatory costs associated with foreign investment vary widely based upon what one newly formed cabinet decides to make law at any given time; the result is an unpredictable and unstable environment for foreign business. 

The RSP campaigned on promises of a predictable multi-year tax environment and a non-partisan governmental agency to administer these laws; if the RSP can deliver a stable institutional environment, Nepal may be able to convince many of its expatriates to invest their money and return their skills to Nepal.

## Conclusion

While the 2026 election was a significant catharsis for a tired nation fed up with rehashed leadership, the true measure of this reformist movement's viability is how well they can move beyond their popular appeal to their spreadsheet appeal as well. The $100 billion dream is a promise that binds the RSP to the Nepalese people. In order to make that promise a reality, the new government must be as ruthless with their economic policies as the people were with their voting choices.
